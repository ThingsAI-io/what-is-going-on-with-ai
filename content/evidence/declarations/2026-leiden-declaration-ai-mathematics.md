# Leiden Declaration on AI and Mathematics: 3,500+ Mathematicians Set Rules for AI Use in the Discipline

## What This Is

The **Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics** is a community statement, published **June 2, 2026** (DOI [10.5281/zenodo.20302944](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20302944)), calling on mathematicians, institutions, funders, policymakers, and the AI industry to manage how artificial intelligence is used in mathematical research. It is **endorsed by the International Mathematical Union (IMU)** — the discipline's leading global body — and had **3,531 individual signatories** as of August 11, 2026, with more added continuously.

It is the work of a **16-member working group** convened by **Jim Portegies** (Eindhoven University of Technology), emerging from a September 2025 conference at Leiden University's Lorentz Center ("Mechanization and Mathematical Research," ~60 participants from 10 countries) and drafted over the following eight months with extensive community feedback. Named signatories to the endorsement include **Terence Tao**, **Peter Scholze**, **Robbert Dijkgraaf**, **Kevin Buzzard**, **Steven Strogatz**, and others. It was officially presented at the **International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia** in July 2026.

The declaration is at once a statement of values, a threat assessment, and a set of actionable recommendations. Its grounding claim is that mathematics is "a profoundly human endeavour" whose characteristic features — proof, attribution, transparency, shared standards, and autonomous research direction — are each endangered by current AI developments. Its headline recommendation to individual mathematicians: **disclose the use of automated tools and retain human responsibility** for the correctness of published mathematics. Its headline recommendations to policymakers: **"Don't believe the hype"** about AI capability claims, regulate the AI industry, and fund public computational infrastructure.

## The Declaration's Structure

### Values the declaration seeks to preserve

The preamble identifies five characteristic values of mathematical research: (1) proof as the highest form of certainty, conveying understanding; (2) results attributable to specific authors who take credit and responsibility; (3) arguments transparent and independently verifiable without proprietary equipment; (4) shared standards for evaluating depth, difficulty, and significance; and (5) the autonomous shaping of research direction by expert communities.

### Potential threats named

The declaration lists five threats, noting they "disproportionately affect students and early-career mathematicians":

1. **Unreliable proofs**: automated techniques can produce "plausible but unreliable (or even incorrect) arguments which are difficult to distinguish from correct mathematical proofs," straining the review system.
2. **Breakdown of attribution**: models trained on the "published mathematical commons" return outputs that fail to cite the human works they synthesize, and are built on data obtained by "systematically exploiting licenses and access arrangements that were not made with artificial intelligence in mind, or indeed by simply violating copyright protections."
3. **Distorted incentives**: AI use (and AI-amenable problems) may be incentivized for its own sake, disrupting hiring, funding, and recognition, disadvantaging researchers without access or without willingness to use tools "controlled by organizations whose values they do not share."
4. **Informal publication**: results communicated via press releases or blog posts before community evaluation, oversimplifying reporting and "misleadingly us[ing] specific mathematical tasks as metrics for the general reasoning capacities of commercial products."
5. **Loss of autonomy**: tech-company involvement may prioritize "research questions... because of their amenability to automated mathematics, rather than expert judgment of their deeper significance."

The declaration ties these to the wider AI moment: "warfare, mass surveillance, political disruption, and environmental damage... By failing to act, we run the risk of becoming complicit in the support of technologies which threaten much more than the practice of mathematics."

### Recommendations, by audience

- **Individual mathematicians**: disclose tool use (a "Tool and computational resource disclosure" section in papers), support reviewing, adhere to open-science principles, retain human responsibility for correctness, affirm the humanity of authorship, put proactive effort into attribution, participate in public discourse, stay informed, welcome new contributors, consider carefully which tools to use (including non-proprietary/energy-efficient alternatives), and evaluate the ethical consequences of their work.
- **Organizations and not-for-profit funders**: build expertise, take the lead on publishing and reviewing policies, maintain standards of rigor (human descriptions of central arguments, formal verification when appropriate, cross-checking), protect the rights of authors — explicitly including that **"material should not be used as training data without consent"** with an **opt-out in publishing agreements** — insist on peer-reviewed venues, support public research laboratories independent of industry, provide frameworks for collaboration (legal representation), and align funding with the declaration's values.
- **Policymakers**: protect the rights of authors, **don't believe the hype** (consult experts rather than press releases), regulate the AI industry ("We stand with others in calling for significantly increased public oversight"), and invest in public computational infrastructure.
- **Commercial AI developers**: the declaration notes mathematics' attractiveness to AI development ("the correctness of formalized proofs can be checked automatically... an effectively unlimited source of feedback for training"), calls for collaborations with industry to abide by its standards, and insists on "freedom of conscience of employees or contributors to speak openly about corporate policies."

## Why It Matters for This Repository

This is the **research-integrity frontier** of the institutionalization wave this repo documents — the mathematicians' counterpart to the [GCC AI licensing policy](../legal/2026-gcc-ai-policy-adoption.md), and to the [economists' "We Must Act Now" statement](2026-we-must-act-now-economists-ai-statement.md), both of which arrived in the same summer of 2026. Where the GCC answers the question *who may contribute code* and the economists the question *how fast can institutions adapt*, the Leiden Declaration answers *what must be preserved in a discipline's practice*: it is a guild-level refusal to let correctness, credit, and autonomy be outsourced.

Two threads tie it directly to the legal evidence in this repo:

- **Training data and the commons.** The declaration's statement that models are built on mathematical publications "or indeed by simply violating copyright protections," and its demand for training-data consent with an opt-out, is the mathematics community's version of the [Richner publishers' C4/WebText allegations](../legal/2026-richner-publishers-v-microsoft-openai.md) and the [News Corp v. Brave](../legal/2026-news-corp-v-brave-crawlers.md) extraction dispute — the same knowledge-commons extraction the courts are now deciding, raised here as a disciplinary standard rather than a legal claim. It connects to [Liang and Lu's](../../bibliography/2026-liang-creative-ownership-ai.md) account of creative ownership of AI-generated work.
- **"Don't believe the hype."** The declaration's warning that math tasks are being used as "metrics for the general reasoning capacities of commercial products" — and that press releases cannot replace peer review — is a research-community challenge to the frontier-marketing dynamic behind the [OpenAI copyright litigation](../legal/2026-openai-copyright-md-l-chatgpt-logs-discovery.md) and the [Sanders letter's](../legal/2026-sanders-letter-ai-ceos-pause-development.md) demand to "pause AI development."

It is also direct evidence for the [productivity J-curve](../../frameworks/productivity-j-curve.md) reading of institutional response: the mathematical community is not waiting for courts or markets — it is building its own guardrails, transparency norms, and public-infrastructure demands at the pace of a single well-attended conference cycle.

## Limits

- **A community statement, not law or measurement.** The declaration contains no data about AI's actual effect on mathematics; it is a values-based normative document. Its threat descriptions are asserted, not quantified.
- **Signatory counts are dynamic.** 3,531 at access (2026-08-11); Oxford reported ~1,500 in the first week; heise reported 3,000+ by July 27. The count rises continuously and is not a stable figure.
- **Self-selected endorsement.** Signing is open to any individual with ORCID/verified email (the list includes hobbyists and students as well as professors); the signature list does not distinguish professional status categories, so "3,500 mathematicians" is an upper bound on the count of professional mathematicians, not a certified figure.
- **Non-binding.** Endorsement by the IMU is a statement of support; the declaration's own recommendations to journals, funders, and governments are exhortations whose adoption is unverified here.
- **The "training without consent" claim is asserted, not adjudicated.** The declaration's copyright language reflects the community's position; the legal outcome of training-data disputes is being decided in the court cases this repo tracks.
