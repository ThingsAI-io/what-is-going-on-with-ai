# Hidden AI Debts of Five US Tech Giants: Nikkei's $1.65tn Estimate

## What This Is

On July 21, 2026, Nikkei Asia published an investigation (author: Kohei Yamada, Palo Alto) estimating that off-balance-sheet commitments at five US technology giants — Meta, Oracle, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Amazon — reached **$1.65 trillion**, up roughly **eightfold in about four years**, exceeding the roughly $1.35 trillion of debt shown on their balance sheets. The article is paywalled; the lede ("Hidden debt at U.S. tech giants swelled eightfold in roughly four years to an estimated $1.65 trillion as artificial intelligence investments ballooned... exceeding actual debt and making it tougher for investors to assess risk") and the subhead ("Data center leases, GPU supply contracts raise liabilities at Meta, Oracle, Nikkei study shows") were confirmed directly.

Because the article itself is behind a paywall, the numbers below are reconstructed from the SEC filings the investigation cites (Meta, Alphabet, Amazon Q1-2026 10-Qs; Microsoft 10-Q for the quarter ended March 31, 2026; Oracle FY2026 10-K) and checked against seven independent analyses that reproduce the headline: CCIR (July 22), Finterm (July 23), MLQ (July 23), TFTC (July 23), Fortune (July 31), Ground Truth (July 21), and a third-party fact-check.

## The Structure of the Obligations

The $1.65tn figure splits almost evenly into two disclosed-but-unrecognized buckets:

- **~$821 billion of leases signed but not yet commenced** — data-center leases where payments begin only when facilities are delivered. Under lease accounting, these sit in the notes, not on the balance sheet, until commencement.
- **~$829 billion of purchase and construction commitments** — contracts to buy chips, power, and cloud capacity, counted at minimums the companies cannot walk away from (non-cancelable, unconditional, or take-or-pay).

Per-company reconstructions (Finterm, from filings):

| Company | Filing basis | Leases (uncommenced) | Purchase/construction | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meta | Mar 31, 2026 | $182.9bn | $237.7bn | **$420.6bn** |
| Alphabet | Mar 31, 2026 | $75.6bn | $332.4bn | **$408.0bn** |
| Microsoft | Mar 2026 / June 2025 | $196.6bn | $142.1bn | **$338.7bn** |
| Oracle | May 31, 2026 | $260.0bn | $13.3bn | **$273.3bn** |

| **Total** | | **$821.4bn** | **$829.2bn** | **$1,650.7bn** |

## Why the Figure Is Disputed in Framing

Independent fact-checks broadly confirm the arithmetic but dispute the label:

- The $1.65tn is **not debt** in the accounting sense. It is an undiscounted stream of future contractual payments. Recognized interest-bearing borrowings across the five were about **$430bn**, not $1.35tn — the $1.35tn figure is total liabilities.
- The obligations are **disclosed** in SEC filings and footnotes; they are not Enron-style concealment. The accurate description is "disclosed but unrecognized future commitments."
- The total is **undiscounted and multi-year** (terms from 1 to 30 years), so comparing it to a same-date balance-sheet stock overstates leverage.
- Not all of it is AI-specific; categories include content, energy, and consumer hardware.

### Fact-check verdict

**Arithmetic corroborated.** Finterm (which paid for the full Nikkei article and reconciled to SEC filings) reproduces the $1.65tn total exactly — ~$821bn of leases not yet commenced plus ~$829bn of purchase/construction commitments — and confirms the "eightfold" growth claim: the $1,650.7bn total is 8.0015× the ~$206.3bn baseline Nikkei reports for fiscal 2022 under the same category set. CCIR independently sums the same buckets to ~$1.67tn, with the small difference attributable to category definitions (mostly how much of Alphabet and Microsoft purchase obligations is counted).

**Label contested, not the numbers.** The "$1.35tn of balance-sheet debt" comparison holds up in magnitude — Finterm confirms total liabilities around $1.35tn — but this is total liabilities, not interest-bearing debt (~$430bn). Calling future commitments "hidden debt" is therefore a framing choice, not a GAAP finding: Enron-style concealment implies undisclosed liabilities and sham entities, neither of which applies. These are disclosed, executory, non-interest-bearing obligations.

**Further caveats confirmed by the fact-checks.**
- Finterm flags mixed reporting dates in the reconstruction (e.g., Microsoft's uncommenced-lease figure dates to March 2026 while its purchase commitments date to June 2025); comparisons at a single point in time are approximate.
- The commitment stream runs 1–30 years and may ultimately be settled as capex/PP&E, inventory, or opex rather than debt.
- Category sets differ across analysts: Morgan Stanley estimates roughly $1tn of purchase commitments alone against Nikkei's ~$829bn, and Alphabet's Q2 2026 filing already pushes the updated total toward ~$2.14tn.
- No company accepted the "hidden debt" framing in the reporting window; the relevant public rebuttals frame the obligations as disclosed backlog-funding commitments tied to contracted revenue.

## Where the Risk Is Genuinely Concentrated

The mechanisms that move real risk off balance sheets:

- **Project finance / SPVs**. A joint venture or special-purpose vehicle develops a data center, borrows privately, and leases it back to a hyperscaler. The BIS (March 2026) calls this **"shadow borrowing"** — obligations economically akin to debt that reside largely outside corporate balance sheets, with private-credit funds, insurers, and banks on the hook. The insurer leg of that chain is state-backed: see the [AI bailout state-backstop evidence entry](2026-ai-bailout-state-backstop-private-credit-life-insurers.md) on PE-owned life insurers and state guaranty funds.
- **Meta's Hyperion venture** is the concrete example: Meta holds 20%, Blue Owl-managed funds 80%, ~$27bn of SPV debt was arranged (Morgan Stanley; PIMCO anchor); Meta leases back the facilities and provides residual-value guarantees up to ~$28bn, with a disclosed maximum exposure to loss of ~$46bn and booked liability near zero. Confirmed at 5GW / >$50bn cost in July 2026.
- **Credit backstops**. Alphabet accounts for data-center payment backstops as credit derivatives (zero at end-2024, $28.4bn at March 2026, +~$15.3bn in April, framework up to $33.3bn) and agreed to ~$35bn of lease-payment support for Anthropic. Amazon holds a $20bn financing facility for Anthropic unlocking as compute milestones are reached.
- **Circular vendor financing**. Nvidia reportedly backstops up to $250bn for OpenAI compute leasing and more in chip purchases; OpenAI's $300bn Oracle contract and ~$1.15tn of vendor commitments raise correlated-risk concerns.

## Offsetting Revenue and the Credit Question

Four of the five companies also sell compute, with disclosed remaining performance obligations (RPO) of about **$2.1tn combined** (Microsoft $633bn, Oracle $638bn, Alphabet $467.6bn, Amazon $364bn) — contracted future revenue that nominally covers the commitments. The asymmetry: the commitments are owed by the five strongest credits in the market, while the backlog is owed by a small set of private, unrated counterparties (notably OpenAI), the same parties the sellers often finance. Amazon explicitly ties its backlog growth to an OpenAI arrangement expanded to $138bn.

## Scale of the Broader AI Credit Cycle

- AI-related debt issuance: ~$236bn globally by May 31, 2026, roughly **4x the 2025 pace**; Morgan Stanley forecasts ~$570bn for 2026.
- Moody's (February 2026) independently estimated $662bn of $969bn total future lease commitments at the five hyperscalers as not yet commenced and off-balance-sheet, equal to 113% of their adjusted debt.
- Oracle is the clearest single case: S&P downgraded it to **BBB-** (one notch above junk) on July 9, 2026, citing AI commitments; its FY2026 capex jumped from $21.2bn to $55.7bn with negative free cash flow of $23.7bn, and its $638bn RPO is carried by a $300bn OpenAI contract.

## What This Evidence Shows

This investigation is the most concrete public documentation of the **credit transformation** behind the AI buildout: public companies disclose the obligations in footnotes while the debt line stays low, and the actual leverage is carried by project vehicles, private-credit funds, and vendor-financing chains. It directly extends the [Microsoft capex evidence entry](2026-microsoft-fy26-q4-earnings.md) (additions to property and equipment nearly doubling) by showing how the same capex is being financed off-balance-sheet.

## Limits

- The Nikkei article is paywalled; only its headline figure, lede, and selected details were confirmed directly from Nikkei. Per-company numbers rely on reconstruction from SEC filings by third-party analysts (CCIR, Finterm, MLQ), which use slightly different category sets and reporting dates.
- "Hidden debt" is an estimate construction, not a GAAP line item; most of the total is disclosed, executory, and not interest-bearing. The arithmetic is corroborated (Finterm reproduces $1,650.7bn and the 8.0015× four-year growth; CCIR sums ~$1.67tn), so all five of the disputed-framing bullets in the section above rest on the fact-check, not on the primary source alone.
- Subsequent disclosures have moved the numbers: Alphabet's Q2 2026 figures raise the five-company total to roughly $2.14tn under Finterm's sensitivity update.
