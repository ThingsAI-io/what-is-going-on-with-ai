# Executive Summary

This paper explains why new general purpose technologies can look disappointing at first and only later reveal their full productivity gains. The key mechanism is intangible investment: firms must spend time and resources on process redesign, software, business-model change, and human capital before the technology can raise measured output.

That means early productivity statistics can understate the true effect of the technology. The accounting problem is not just delay; it is mismeasurement. Intangible complements are real assets, but they are often not fully captured in standard national accounts while they are being created.

The result is the productivity J-curve: an early dip or slowdown, followed by recovery and, eventually, overperformance once the benefits of the intangible stock are harvested.

## Core Contribution

The paper does two things.

First, it offers a conceptual model of how GPTs interact with intangible investment. A firm that adopts AI or another GPT often has to reorganize workflows, retrain workers, build software, and redesign products before the technology can pay off. Those expenditures crowd out current output while creating future capital.

Second, it uses that model to reinterpret productivity measurement. The authors show that standard measures can be too low early in the adoption cycle and too high later, because the hidden stock of intangibles is not fully visible at the right time.

That makes the J-curve a timing model as much as a productivity model. It helps distinguish temporary adoption costs from longer-run transformation.

## Relevance to This Repository

This paper is useful because it gives the repository a disciplined way to talk about AI productivity without falling into either hype or disappointment.

It supports a key possibility in the project's model layer: AI may be generating short-run disruption, reorganization, and measurement lag rather than immediate broad-based gains. That matters for firms, labor markets, and national statistics alike.

It also connects directly to the repo's other themes. The J-curve helps explain why AI can improve work at the firm level while still looking weak in aggregate data, and why complementary investments and institutional adjustment should be treated as part of the technology's effect, not as background noise.

## What the Source Leaves Open

The paper is strongest as a mechanism for productivity measurement and technology adoption. It is weaker as a full theory of social transformation.

It does not by itself tell us who captures the gains, how the gains are distributed across workers or countries, or when the short-run dip turns into a permanent loss instead of a rebound. Those questions need other sources in the bibliography.
