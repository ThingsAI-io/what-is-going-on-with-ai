# AI and the Global Productivity Divide: Fuel for the Fast or a Lift for the Laggards?

## Executive Summary

This OECD working paper examines whether AI will narrow or widen the global productivity gap between high-income countries (HICs) and low-income/lower-middle-income countries (LICs/LMICs). Using a three-component framework — AI adoption rates, AI exposure (share of tasks AI can improve), and AI task-level gains — the authors systematically assess the structural characteristics of LICs/LMICs that determine their capacity to benefit from AI-driven productivity growth.

The core finding is alarming: structural weaknesses in LICs and LMICs are likely to outweigh any advantages, making AI a force that deepens rather than bridges the global productivity divide. On adoption, LICs/LMICs face compounding barriers: 59% of LIC populations have no formal schooling (effectively excluding them from AI tools requiring literacy), 53% lack electricity, digital infrastructure is underdeveloped (fixed broadband penetration at 0.5 per 100 in LICs), and the cost of AI tools is prohibitive (ChatGPT Plus costs ~25% of average annual income in LICs). On exposure, AI's productivity benefits concentrate in knowledge-intensive services, which represent a small fraction of LIC/LMIC economies compared to agriculture and low-skill manufacturing. AI exposure in LICs is roughly half that of HICs. On task-level gains, the evidence is mixed: AI benefits lower-skilled workers within specific occupations, but does not enable workers in low-qualification professions to perform high-skill tasks.

The paper identifies one comparative advantage for LICs/LMICs — a younger workforce (75% under 40 in LICs, vs. ~50% in HICs), since younger workers adopt AI faster — but estimates this yields only 5-10% higher adoption, far from compensating for structural barriers. The paper also examines the leapfrogging hypothesis (can LICs skip earlier technologies and adopt AI directly?) and finds it unlikely: unlike mobile telephony, AI adoption correlates with adoption of other digital technologies, suggesting sequential infrastructure development remains necessary.

The policy recommendations focus on four short-term priorities — energy and digital infrastructure, workforce skills and education, access to finance, and institutional/regulatory preparedness — and two long-term strategies: leveraging AI for human capital development (health and education applications) and exploiting trade and global value chain integration as channels for technology spillovers.

## Key Contributions

1. **Three-Component Analytical Framework Applied Globally**: Adapts Acemoglu's task-based framework (adoption × exposure × task-level gains) to analyze heterogeneous AI impacts across country income levels, providing the first systematic comparison across all three dimensions for LICs/LMICs vs. HICs.

2. **Quantified Adoption Barriers**: Provides specific data points that make abstract "digital divide" discussions concrete — 59% no schooling in LICs, 53% without electricity, ChatGPT Plus at 25% of annual income, 4G coverage at 17% in rural LICs, fixed broadband at 0.5/100.

3. **Leapfrogging Debunked for AI**: Using World Bank evidence, demonstrates that while leapfrogging occurred for mobile telephony, it failed for advanced production automation — and AI resembles the latter (dependent on complementary infrastructure and organizational change) rather than the former.

4. **Exposure Gap Quantification**: Shows AI exposure in LICs is approximately half of HICs, driven by industrial structure (agriculture at 42% of African employment vs. knowledge-intensive services dominating HICs), establishing exposure as a structural constraint beyond what adoption policy can address in the short run.

5. **India/Philippines Exception Model**: Identifies ICT/business services export specialization as a viable pathway for selected LMICs to achieve high AI exposure despite lower income levels, suggesting GVC integration as a mechanism for AI-driven development.

6. **Task-Level Equalization Myth Check**: Clarifies that while AI boosts lower-skilled workers within specific occupations, it does not enable cross-occupation skill substitution — countering narratives that AI could serve as a "great equalizer" across the global workforce.

## Relevance to Topics

### AI Impact on Economy, Labor, and Knowledge Production (`ai-economy-labor`)

This paper provides the macro-level, cross-country productivity framework that complements the within-country and within-profession analyses in the bibliography. Where other entries examine how AI restructures specific labor markets (software engineering, UX design, science organizations), this paper asks whether AI restructures the global economy — and concludes that it likely widens the divide. The finding that AI productivity gains range from 0.05-0.3pp annually in low-complementary-factor countries versus 0.5-1.5pp in the US quantifies the divergence risk. The India/Philippines exception demonstrates that GVC positioning can override income-level disadvantages, but only for countries with specific sectoral specializations.

### AI and Learning and Education (`ai-learning-education`)

The paper identifies education as both a barrier to AI adoption and a channel through which AI could support long-term convergence. The dual finding — that 59% of LIC populations lack the schooling to use AI tools, but that AI tutoring can be cost-effective in low-resource settings — creates a chicken-and-egg problem: the populations most in need of AI-enhanced education are least able to access it. The paper cites evidence that AI can personalize instruction and deliver higher gains for low-proficiency learners, but warns that incentivizing AI use for short-term test performance could harm long-term skill acquisition.

### AI Ethics and Safety, Governance, Alignment (`ai-ethics-safety`)

The paper addresses governance through the lens of regulatory preparedness: LICs/LMICs tend to have lower AI regulatory capacity, increasing risks of consumer distrust (fraud, bias, unreliability). It also raises equity concerns — AI adoption could exacerbate within-country inequalities in Africa, where urban digital hubs coexist with rural areas lacking basic connectivity. The OECD AI principles are positioned as a framework for international cooperation to promote trustworthy AI across income levels.

## Methodology

This is a qualitative policy analysis paper, not an empirical study. It employs:
- **Task-based analytical framework** from Acemoglu (2025) and Filippucci, Gal & Schief (2024): decomposing productivity impact into adoption × exposure × task-level gains
- **Cross-country comparative analysis** using World Bank income group classifications (LIC, LMIC, UMIC, HIC)
- **Descriptive statistics** from multiple data sources: IMF AI Preparedness Index, Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index, ChatGPT adoption data, ITU digital infrastructure statistics, World Bank economic structure data
- **Literature synthesis** of emerging research on AI productivity effects, technology adoption in developing economies, and task-level AI performance
- **Case studies**: Mobile telephony leapfrogging vs. advanced automation non-leapfrogging; India and Philippines as LMIC AI adoption exceptions; Africa as a focus region

## Key Themes and Findings

### 1. The Three-Barrier Model for AI Adoption in LICs/LMICs

The paper identifies three compounding barriers:
- **Skills/education**: Literacy prerequisite for AI tool use; 59% of LIC population has no schooling; English proficiency as an additional barrier (Claude's accuracy drops 21-54% in Yoruba)
- **Infrastructure**: Electricity access (47% in LICs), broadband penetration (0.5/100 in LICs), data center absence (no major LLM providers run data centers in Africa), latency from connecting to distant cloud services
- **Cost**: ChatGPT Plus at ~25% of annual LIC income; credit constraints preventing firm-level systemic adoption; limited access to finance for complementary investments

### 2. Exposure as a Structural Constraint

AI's productivity benefits concentrate in knowledge-intensive services. LICs/LMICs have unfavorable sectoral compositions (high agriculture, low-skill manufacturing, low knowledge-intensive services). This is not addressable through short-term policy — changing sectoral composition requires long-term structural transformation. AI exposure in LICs is roughly half of HICs.

### 3. The Leapfrogging Question

The paper provides nuanced evidence:
- **Mobile telephony leapfrogged** fixed-line infrastructure (laggard firms adopted mobile despite never using fixed-line)
- **Advanced production automation did NOT leapfrog** earlier digital technologies (computers and internet remained prerequisites)
- **AI resembles advanced automation** more than mobile telephony: requires complementary infrastructure, organizational change, and prior digital adoption
- Early evidence from HICs confirms: AI adoption correlates significantly with adoption of other digital technologies

### 4. Long-Run Convergence Channels

Two potential pathways:
- **Human capital enhancement**: AI applications in health and education could improve workforce quality in LICs/LMICs, but requires careful evaluation of unintended negative effects (e.g., skill acquisition impairment from AI shortcuts)
- **Trade and knowledge spillovers**: GVC participation, FDI, and open-source AI platforms as vehicles for technology diffusion; but benefits are limited if domestic AI adoption remains low (cheaper imports offset by less competitive exports)

### 5. The India/Philippines Model

These LMICs demonstrate that ICT/business services export specialization can override income-level disadvantages for AI adoption. Their AI usage relative to income exceeds even HICs, driven by high-exposure sectoral composition (ICT and services exports as significant GDP shares). This suggests GVC climbing as a viable strategy for selected countries with appropriate skill endowments.

## Relevance to This Repository

This paper expands the repository beyond firm-level and occupational evidence by showing that AI transformation is also a geography problem. The project is trying to build a model of what is going on with AI across economy, society, and work, and Chaar makes it clear that adoption, exposure, and gains are mediated by education, infrastructure, sectoral mix, and cost.

That matters for the repo's model layer because it prevents a HIC-centric view from masquerading as a universal one. It also helps the evidence layer: the same AI system may be a productivity booster in one country, a weak tool in another, and a non-event in a third. Any serious synthesis has to explain those divergences.

## Open Questions and Further Research

1. Can targeted satellite internet (Starlink, etc.) overcome the last-mile connectivity barrier at scale in LICs, or will cost and coverage limitations maintain the divide?
2. How do the India/Philippines exception cases translate into policy lessons — is ICT export specialization replicable for other LMICs, or does it depend on unique historical conditions (English proficiency, HIC offshoring relationships)?
3. What is the empirical relationship between AI adoption and leapfrogging vs. sequential technology adoption in LICs — can future AI architectures (edge computing, on-device inference) change the infrastructure dependency?
4. How do AI-enhanced education and health interventions perform in rigorous long-term evaluations in LIC settings — do short-term gains in test performance or diagnostics translate into sustained human capital improvements?
5. What role does open-source AI play in lowering cost barriers for LICs/LMICs — does the rise of DeepSeek and other lower-cost models materially change the affordability picture?
