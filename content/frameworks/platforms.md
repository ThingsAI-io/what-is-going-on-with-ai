# Platforms

## In a nutshell

In plain language, a **platform** is a stable base that other people or firms can build on.

It is not just a product. It is a product, technology, or service **plus** a set of interfaces, rules, and incentives that make complementary activity possible.

The simplest way to explain it is this:

- a platform provides a core;
- other actors build complements around that core;
- the more useful complements exist, the more valuable the core becomes;
- the more valuable the core becomes, the more people want to use it;
- the more people use it, the more attractive it becomes for others to build on it.

That recursive loop is why platforms can become economically powerful.

The framework matters because it helps us see that some phenomena are not best understood as isolated products or firms. They are better understood as **systems of coordination**. A platform organizes relationships between a core and a changing set of complements, users, suppliers, developers, distributors, or other participants. It shapes who can innovate, who depends on whom, how value is created, and how power accumulates.

If someone asks what a platform is, a good short answer is:

> A platform is a stable core with interfaces that allow others to build complements, creating a wider ecosystem whose growth increases the value of the core.

## Basic Definition

In the Gawer and Cusumano tradition, a platform is not merely a standalone artifact. It is a structured arrangement with at least four parts:

1. A **core** that performs an essential function.
2. A set of **interfaces** or connection points that allow other components or actors to attach to the core.
3. A surrounding field of **complements** that extend the value of the core.
4. A form of **governance** that determines how the whole system is coordinated.

The core can be a product, a technology, a service, a standard-bearing architecture, or some combination of these. The complements can be products, applications, services, modules, content, accessories, or participating market sides. The platform exists when the core is designed or positioned in such a way that others can build on it rather than merely consume it.

This is the point where platform thinking departs from ordinary product thinking. A normal product is sold and used. A platform is used, but it also becomes a base for further activity by others.

## The Key Components of a Platform

### The Core

The core is the stable element around which other activity is organized. It performs an essential function and remains relatively durable compared with the surrounding variety.

The core matters because it reduces uncertainty. Participants in the ecosystem can invest in complements only if they believe the core will remain stable enough to build on.

### Interfaces

Interfaces are the points through which others connect to the core. They may be technical specifications, APIs, standards, design rules, compatibility rules, contracts, or procedural norms.

Interfaces matter because they convert the core from a closed object into an extensible system. Without interfaces, there may be a product, but not a platform in the strong sense.

### Complements

Complements are the products, services, modules, or activities that make the platform more useful.

The core often has limited value on its own. The surrounding complements are what make it compelling. This is why platforms often depend on third-party innovation, not just internal production.

### Governance

Governance is the set of rules and decisions that shapes how participation happens.

Governance includes questions such as:

- Who is allowed to build on the platform?
- Under what technical rules?
- At what cost?
- With what constraints?
- Who captures which share of value?
- What happens when the platform owner also competes with complementors?

Governance is central because platforms are not neutral spaces. They are structured and steered.

## Platform Thinking Versus Product Thinking

Platform thinking begins when we stop asking only, "What is this product?" and start asking, "What can others build on top of this?"

In product thinking, value comes mainly from the qualities of the finished object.

In platform thinking, value comes from the interaction between:

- the quality of the core,
- the ease of connection,
- the incentives for complementors,
- and the size and vitality of the surrounding ecosystem.

This means that a platform owner often has to make different strategic choices than an ordinary producer. It may need to open parts of its system, subsidize participation, stabilize interfaces, and tolerate a degree of external autonomy in order to expand the whole ecosystem.

## The Main Distinction: Product Platforms and Industry Platforms

Cusumano makes a crucial distinction between **product platforms** and **industry platforms**, and Gawer extends it.

### Product Platforms

A product platform is an internal architectural base used to create a family of related products.

The classic logic is reuse. A firm designs common components or a shared structure so that it can produce many variants more efficiently. The main benefits are lower development costs, faster derivative creation, and greater variety with less duplication.

This is still important, but it is the narrower meaning of the term.

### Industry Platforms

An industry platform is a core that multiple external actors can build upon.

Here the logic is not just reuse. It is ecosystem formation. The platform owner does not merely coordinate its own internal product variants. It enables third parties to innovate, connect, and create additional value around the core.

This is the richer and more strategically important meaning of the platform concept.

## Gawer's Three Levels of Platforms

Gawer pushes the framework further by distinguishing three contexts in which platform logic appears.

### Internal Platforms

Internal platforms exist within firms. They are architectures for product families and internal reuse.

The central questions are:

- how much commonality should be built in;
- how much variation should be allowed;
- and how to balance efficiency with flexibility.

### Supply Chain Platforms

Supply chain platforms extend this logic across firms that are linked in production.

Here the issue is not only architecture, but also coordination between partners. The platform spans organizational boundaries, so interfaces, responsibilities, incentives, and relational governance become more important.

### Industry Platforms

Industry platforms become the base for a wider ecosystem of firms that are not simply linked in one supply chain.

At this point, the platform is no longer just an engineering device. It becomes an economic and social structure. It organizes markets, allocates opportunities, creates dependencies, and shapes the direction of innovation.

This three-level framework is important because it shows that "platform" does not describe one single phenomenon. It describes related but distinct arrangements whose scale and governance become progressively broader.

## Economic Mechanics of Platforms

### Complementarity

The first economic mechanism is complementarity. The platform core becomes more valuable when useful complements are available. The complements become more valuable when the core is widely used.

This reciprocal relation is the basis of platform growth.

### Network Effects

Platforms often benefit from **network effects**, though not every platform effect is the same.

Direct network effects occur when the value rises because more users join the same network.

Indirect network effects occur when the value rises because more participants attract more complements, which then attract more users, and so on.

Indirect network effects are especially important in platform systems because they connect the growth of one part of the ecosystem to the growth of another.

### Positive Feedback Loops

Complementarity and network effects produce positive feedback loops.

The core attracts complements.
Complements attract users.
Users attract more complementors.
The expanded ecosystem makes the core more valuable.

These loops help explain why platforms can scale quickly and why established platforms can become difficult to dislodge.

### Switching Costs and Lock-in

As platforms accumulate complements and habits of use, switching away from them can become costly.

These costs may be technical, organizational, economic, or social. The result is that competition becomes less about isolated product comparison and more about the cost of moving between ecosystems.

### Multi-sided Coordination

Platforms often bring together multiple groups whose participation depends on one another. Users want complements. Complementors want users. Service providers want installed bases. Advertisers want attention. Sellers want buyers.

This means the platform owner often has to solve a coordination problem before the platform becomes self-sustaining. Subsidies, access rules, bundling, and openness are often used to get the ecosystem moving.

## Social and Organizational Meaning of Platforms

Platforms are not only economic mechanisms. They are also organizational forms.

They shape the division of labor between a core and a periphery.
They determine which actors specialize in the base system and which specialize in complements.
They allocate decision rights.
They structure dependency.
They determine where experimentation is encouraged and where stability is enforced.

In that sense, a platform is a way of organizing collective innovation.

This is one of Gawer's most important contributions: platform thinking is not just about market structure, and not just about technical structure. It is about the relation between the two.

The architecture of the system influences the architecture of the industry.

## Standards Are Not the Same as Platforms

Cusumano insists on a useful distinction: **standards are not platforms**.

Standards are rules that allow interoperability. They matter, but they are only part of the picture.

A platform includes standards or design rules, but it also includes:

- a core around which activity is organized,
- a set of complements,
- and a governance structure that shapes participation and value capture.

Confusing standards with platforms makes the concept too thin. A standard can exist without supporting a vibrant ecosystem. A platform, by contrast, is defined by its capacity to organize complementary activity.

## Openness and Control

One of the deepest tensions in the framework is the tension between **openness** and **control**.

Platforms need openness because outside actors must be able to connect, innovate, and invest.

But platforms also need control because the core must remain coherent, stable, and governable.

If a platform is too closed, complementors lose incentive to participate.
If it is too open, the core may lose coherence, differentiation, or profitability.

This means that platforms are always political in a broad sense: they involve decisions about access, dependency, priority, and appropriation.

## Platform Leadership

Platform leadership is not simply having the best product.

It is the ability to:

- define a core others rely on;
- make connection to that core possible;
- stimulate complementary innovation;
- maintain ecosystem confidence;
- and preserve enough influence over the system's direction.

This is why Cusumano and Gawer both treat platform strategy as a distinct managerial problem. The platform leader is not only a producer. It is also an architect, coordinator, rule-setter, and broker.

## Platform Emergence as an Evolutionary Process

Gawer's evolutionary view is especially useful because it treats platform formation as a process rather than a given.

A system may start as an internal architecture.
That architecture may extend outward into a supply chain.
As external capabilities and complementarities grow, it may become an industry platform.

This means a platform is not simply designed once. It is often produced through cumulative changes in design, organization, capability distribution, and market structure.

This perspective helps explain why platforms can feel both technical and social at once: they are built in technology, but they mature through relationships.

## What to Look For in a Real-World Phenomenon

To identify a platform in practice, ask these questions:

1. Is there a stable core that performs an essential function?
2. Can others build complements around it?
3. Are there interfaces or rules that make such connection possible?
4. Does the value of the core depend significantly on the presence of complements?
5. Does growth create feedback loops between users and complementors?
6. Is there a governance problem about openness, access, or control?
7. Does the arrangement shape a wider ecosystem rather than just one product?

The more often the answer is yes, the more likely it is that you are dealing with a platform phenomenon.

## Common Mistakes

Several mistakes recur when people use the word "platform."

One mistake is to call any successful product a platform.
Another is to treat a technical standard as if it were already a platform.
Another is to ignore governance and talk only about network effects.
Another is to focus on the platform core and forget that complements are what often create the system's practical value.

The framework is most useful when all four dimensions remain visible at once:

- core,
- interfaces,
- complements,
- governance.

## Bottom Line

The platform framework, as clarified by Gawer and Cusumano, explains a specific kind of economic and social phenomenon: a system in which a stable core enables complementary activity by others, and where the resulting ecosystem becomes the main source of value and power.

Its importance lies in showing that some forms of competition and coordination are not best understood firm by firm or product by product. They are better understood as struggles over architecture, interfaces, participation, and ecosystem control.

That is what makes platform thinking a framework rather than just a buzzword.
